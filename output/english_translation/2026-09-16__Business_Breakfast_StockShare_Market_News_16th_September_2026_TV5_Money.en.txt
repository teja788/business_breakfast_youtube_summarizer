The government and NPCI have created another controversy by introducing a merchant discount rate, or MDR, on UPI. For six years, UPI payments were free. Because there was no commission, all of us shifted even ordinary bank payments to UPI. Everyone knew fees would eventually have to be charged, but announcing them in an already negative environment makes the timing look poor. Person-to-person transactions remain free--for example, a payment from me to a friend. Merchant payments up to Rs 2,000 also remain free, such as buying something from a paan shop or paying for coconut water. Above Rs 2,000, the merchant charge will be 0.4%, capped at Rs 300 per transaction. Some categories such as capital-market and mutual-fund transactions may carry a nominal 0.02%, not 0.4%. If a mutual fund is bought through a broker, the broker pays that service charge and then decides whether to absorb it or pass it to the customer.

The system could earn roughly Rs 22,000 crore, more than $2 billion--a substantial amount for the banking and financial system. This is positive for payment companies such as Pine Labs, Paytm, PhonePe, Google Pay, Quick/Mobile Quick and MobiKwik, and banks such as Yes Bank, which supplies many merchant payment terminals and may be a major beneficiary. Banks may share part of the 0.4% with merchants or collect it from them. Until now people had largely forgotten NEFT and RTGS, but they may return to those channels when UPI carries a transaction charge. The banks collect only a nominal Rs 5-10 there, so a user may prefer paying the bank directly. Keep Yes Bank on today's stock watch list.

Yesterday the broader market corrected by more than 1.5%. When markets fall, investors book profits wherever they still have profits, and defence and pharma had risen sharply. There was a spell of panic selling in which one person's fear triggered another's. SMS Pharma, which we discussed only recently, fell more than 12%. Such situations occur occasionally. An occasional jolt is healthy and necessary because it prevents investors from becoming indifferent and assuming that nothing bad can happen. During indiscriminate selling, good stocks also suffer unnecessary collateral damage; those are the names on which we should focus.

A US congressional study estimates that five months of the US-Iran war cost taxpayers $38 billion, around Rs 3.5 lakh crore. Each additional month could cost $2-3 billion. Trump is promising $5,000 to each adult, but midterm voters may weigh that inducement against the war burden. The study expects core CPI to rise by 0.3% and overall inflation by 0.5%. Iran's defence minister says the US lost many missiles and aircraft and that hundreds of US military bases were damaged, calling it only the tip of the iceberg. Trump nevertheless says everything is fine.

With US yields near 5%, hot money will not normally leave America to invest in other markets, particularly when emerging markets such as India face confusion. Domestic money has supported pharma, defence and engineering, but some profit booking can occur there too. We cannot say that a huge fall is certain, but a normal correction in midcaps and small caps can be 15%, 20% or even 30%. The present global tensions make such a correction possible, though no one can say it must happen now.

Prepare a list instead of panicking. First, data-center-related stocks: whatever the OpenAI chief or Anthropic's CEO says, AI is here to stay, and India's AI opportunity is primarily in data-center infrastructure. Also focus on power, health care and pharma. Chemicals deserve attention because the China-plus-one factor is beginning to work. Midcap IT can offer an opportunity because whenever the anti-IT trade reaches an extreme, this part of the sector can strengthen. A stronger dollar normally lowers gold and silver prices, which helps jewellery companies through cheaper inputs. The same move is negative for gold-financing companies. A stronger dollar also weakens base metals, so be alert with metal stocks.
